ChatGPT for Teens. What Parents Should Know

By Donna Duarte-Ladd Posted on October 7, 2026

At a Glance:

  • For ages 13–17: ChatGPT for Teens comes with additional safety protections designed specifically for teenagers.
  • Parents can set boundaries, quiet hours, study hours, voice mode, image generation, group chats, and memory can all be managed through parental controls.
  • Study Mode can walk teens through problems instead of simply handing over an answer.
  • Parents don’t need to be AI experts. Ask what your teen is using it for, set family expectations, and keep talking.

If you have a teenager, AI is part of their world. Let’s be real: AI is so ingrained in their lives that we parents are living in an archaic, yesterday-era world; even if you think you are up to date, they are on a different level, whether we like it or not.  

Some (people) may be okay with how AI is changing the world. And this is not a judgy piece; this is a let’s BE REAL: for us as parents, how it affects and even shapes our children is a concern for many. Maybe they are using it for homework, asking it questions, brainstorming, or doing something you have no idea about. That is why Open for Teens hosted a Parent Forum, moderated by Meaghan Murphy, Editor-in-Chief of Woman’s Day, to share the why and what to expect with ChatGPT for Teens, a version of ChatGPT designed specifically for kids ages 13 to 17.

The new experience which launched in August comes with additional protections for teens, as well as tools for parents to set boundaries around how and when ChatGPT is used.

At this panel, Dr. Michael Rich, director and founder of the Digital Wellness Lab at Boston Children’s Hospital, moderated a conversation with Emily Oster, founder of ParentData; Stephanie LeBlanc-Godfrey, founder of Mother AI; and Dr. Alison Mishkin, Head of Child Development at OpenAI.

During this fireside chat, one topic kept coming back to the discussion: we can’t really separate our kids from the digital world they live in. So how do we help them use these tools in a healthy way?

Oster talked about approaching AI with honesty and acknowledging that parents are figuring this out, too. There is still a lot we don’t know, including exactly what overuse or overreliance on AI might mean for kids. Her advice was to set expectations, create boundaries, and, perhaps most importantly, talk to your kids about what they are actually using AI for.

LeBlanc-Godfrey also encouraged parents to start from a place of curiosity. Ask questions. What are they using it for? What do they find helpful? What don’t they trust? That conversation can make it easier to create family rules without shutting the conversation down. There’s no neat way to wrap all of this up. AI, especially for us parents, is an open-ended topic and will remain so.

So what does ChatGPT for Teens look like?

What to Expect From ChatGPT for Teens

Designed for ages 13 to 17. Teens have additional protections turned on by default. OpenAI says its system will use a combination of age declarations and other signals to determine whether someone may be under 18. If it isn’t confident about a user’s age, it will default to the safer teen experience.

More protections around sensitive content. The teen version is designed to reduce exposure to things like sexual or romantic content, graphic violence, self-harm, and dangerous activities. It also won’t engage in romantic language with teens.

Study Mode. This is one of the bigger focuses of the teen experience. Rather than simply giving a student an answer, Study Mode can break a problem down into steps, ask questions, and help a student work through the material. Teens can also use quizzes, uploaded notes, and visual explanations for subjects such as math and science.

Study Hours. Parents can choose specific times for Study Hours. During those times, Study Mode will be turned on by default.

Homework reminders. If ChatGPT thinks a teen is trying to use it to simply get an assignment done, reminders can redirect them toward Study Mode.

Quiet Hours. Parents can set times when ChatGPT can’t be accessed. There are also break reminders designed to encourage teens to step away after extended conversations.

Parental controls. Parents and teens can link their accounts. Parents can then adjust certain settings, including Study Hours, Quiet Hours, voice mode, image generation, group chats, and memory. Parents do not have access to their teen’s conversations.

Safety notifications. Parents can opt into notifications if OpenAI’s systems and trained reviewers detect possible signs of a serious safety concern. Parents receive information intended to help them support their teen, not access to the teen’s full conversation.

No ads. ChatGPT for Teens will not show ads.

More help with learning. On the horizon, OpenAI is also planning tools around things like college planning and college advising. College Planner will share information on application requirements, deadlines, and help with financial aid steps.

You’re the Human

One point that came up during the panel was the importance of remembering that ChatGPT isn’t a person. Teens may use it to work through a problem, ask for advice, or even talk through a conflict, but we, as parents, can help them understand where AI is useful and where a real person is the better choice.

On our end, parents still have a lot to decide for themselves. How much AI is too much? When is it helping a child learn, and when is it doing the learning for them? What should the rules be around homework? Should there be limits on when it can be used?

There isn’t one answer for every family.

And, as several of the panelists pointed out, parents don’t have to have all the answers. Starting the conversation, asking questions, and figuring out what works for your family is a good place to begin.
