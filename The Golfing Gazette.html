Jacob Bridgeman reveals his best advice for green reading after becoming the best putter on the PGA Tour

Jordan Harris

Jacob Bridgeman is off to a fantastic start as he looks to secure his third victory of the PGA Tour season, with the American leading the Baycurrent Classic after his opening round at Yokohama.

Bridgeman made eight birdies in an opening round of 63 in Japan to set the early pace. Of course, he won on his previous start on the PGA Tour, clinching the inaugural Biltmore Championship Asheville title ahead of securing the winning point at the Presidents Cup.

There is no question that the true strength of the 26-year-old’s game is his putting. He is first on the PGA Tour in strokes gained on the green this year. In fact, putting is the only strokes gained category in which Bridgeman finds himself inside the top-30 on tour.

Jacob Bridgeman shares his best putting advice after becoming the best on the PGA Tour

No one on the PGA Tour has a better conversion rate from inside five feet than Bridgeman, who has made more than 93 per cent of his putts from that distance.

And speaking after his opening round in Japan, Bridgeman explained how he likes to practice his putting.

“I tend to kind of go away from gadgets and drills and everything. I try and do all my practice in like real life scenarios instead of just getting on the putting green and hitting a bunch of the same short putts over and over and working on my stroke,” he said.

“I try to do real life putts and go through a full routine and keep it simple. I like to use my imagination on putting, kind of see the line that I’m going to putt it on and follow the path. I could make it a little bit more complicated if you wanted.”

How Jacob Bridgeman practices green-reading

Of course, having a good putting stroke is all well and good, but that is only part of the challenge when it comes to finding the bottom of the cup.

The pressure on certain putts during a round is difficult to replicate. Meanwhile, it is hugely frustrating when a player hits a good putt but has misread the green.

Green-reading is an underrated skill. Obviously, plenty of players now prefer to use Aimpoint to help them decide how a putt is going to break.

But when asked how he practices reading greens, Bridgeman revealed how important it is to take in as much information as possible when the opportunities present themselves.

“I would say the practice I use for green reading is just watching putts. So the more that I can putt and watch the ball roll, the more I can watch other people putt and watch it roll, I think that is creating a bank of memories of how the ball will roll on greens and what putts look like. I don’t think I have any certain specific practice for green reading but just watching a lot of putts,” he said.

Bridgeman’s challenge will now be to take his form this year into 2027. Unfortunately for him, his results this year will not mathematically help him earn a spot on the Ryder Cup team at Adare Manor.

But if he remains one of the best putters on the PGA Tour in 12 months, he will surely stand a great chance of going to Ireland, whether he qualifies automatically or convinces Jim Furyk to pick him.
